Hatchlings & Heroes

Designing a magical monster-collecting experience for young players.

A comprehensive UX/UI case study on crafting an enchanting companion journal interface. Designed to spark wonder, ensure child-friendly accessibility, and build sticky gamification loops.

01 — The Challenge

How do you create a monster-collecting game that feels exciting and rewarding for children while remaining simple, safe and engaging?

The challenge was to design a game that balances fun, education and progression - with intuitive navigation, clear feedback and a system that keeps young players coming back.

02 — My Design Goals

Playful Learning

Educational mini-games that feel like games not schoolwork.

Emotionally present

Keep the hatchling visible and emotionally present during care and customization.

Accessible Interaction

Large buttons, clear visuals and child-friendly flow.

Rewarding Progression

Every action gives a sense of achievement.

Simple to Understand

Make the hatchling’s identity and current condition understandable at a glance.

03 — The UX Problem

Several specific problems emerged during iteration:

The Hatchling Profile had gradually accumulated care information, battle statistics, personality details, requests, customization controls, equipment actions, and animated effects. Although each feature had value, presenting too much at once made the experience feel more like a data screen than a relationship with a living companion.

The central UX insight was that the hatchling needed to remain the clear focal point while information and actions formed understandable supporting layers around it.

Broken Hierarchy

Important information lacked a clear hierarchy. Identity, care needs, combat statistics, actions, and secondary details competed for attention.

Intrusive Speech Bubbles

Multiple speech bubbles could appear around the hatchling simultaneously. These overlapped the stage and weakened the intended emotional connection.

Viewport Obstructions

Decorations and effects sometimes obstructed the hatchling artwork instead of supporting it.

Low Customization Trust

Makeup Studio interactions were not consistently trustworthy. Touch points could appear in the wrong position, long brush strokes became slow, unsaved work could be lost during navigation, and saving Studio paint could overwrite makeup applied from Inventory.

Artwork Inconsistency

Artwork inconsistencies occasionally made an owned hatchling appear unavailable or invisible in another context, disrupting continuity between the profile and the rest of the game.

08 — Iteration and Problem to Solution

Evolving the hatchling diary interface from flat, chaotic text blocks into an intuitive, layered system fit for master creature-keepers.

BEFORE

The Problem

  • Visual Clutter: Overwhelming:
  • The profile needed to communicate personality, element, mood, health, level, experience, combat statistics, care needs, Bond, evolution readiness, and available actions.
  • Giving every item equal visual emphasis made it difficult to know where to look first.
AFTER

The Solution

  • I reorganized the experience into layers. The top panel now establishes identity through the hatchling’s name, element, mood, health state, rarity, evolution stage, and current thought or request.
  • The hatchling artwork occupies the main stage. Below it, frequently used actions—Food, Toys, Potions, and Equipment—form a consistent action row.
  • Battle progression and care needs are separated into two compact panels. Less frequently needed information, such as fears, species, special power, and evolution requirements, sits behind an expandable Hatchling Details control.
Result

THE OUTCOME

Players can scan the profile in a natural order: who the hatchling is, how it feels, what it needs, and what actions are available. Detailed information remains accessible without dominating the everyday care experience.

09 — One clear communication area

The Problem: Stage Noise

Personality thoughts, active requests, and automatic speech could produce multiple bubbles on the stage. The bubbles competed with one another, covered artwork, and made the hatchling’s communication feel noisy.

  • Overlapping bubbles clipping into UI controls
  • Redundant, spammy duplicate hatchling state lines

The Solution: Unified Log

I consolidated personality thoughts and active requests into one compact speech area inside the identity panel. Stage-level duplicates were removed, and the hatchling renderer was instructed not to create an additional automatic bubble in this context. 

Decorations were placed in a lower visual layer so they remain part of the room without covering the hatchling.

  • Chronological dynamic scroll queue system
  • Automated alert de-duplication with counts
  • Categorized color filters for Spell vs Growth logs

The Result: Flawless Nursery Coordination

The hatchling now has one predictable place to communicate. Its artwork remains visible, requests are easier to read, and the stage feels calmer and more intentional.

10 — Visual and Interaction

A closer look at two key issues and how they were solved.

More trustworthy customization

The Problem

The Makeup Studio depended on precise direct manipulation, but touch coordinates could become misaligned with the visible canvas. Brush movement also created many repeated image points, causing lag during longer strokes. Players could additionally lose unfinished work by leaving the screen, while saving Studio strokes could remove makeup previously applied from Inventory.

The Solution

Touch input was mapped to the measured local paint surface and rejected when outside the valid canvas. Paint remained clipped to the hatchling silhouette. Small pointer movements were filtered, existing paint stamps were prevented from rerendering unnecessarily, and a generous safety limit protected long sessions without visibly shortening normal strokes. 

I also added a leave confirmation with Save and Leave, Keep Editing, and Discard choices. Studio saves now merge new strokes with the hatchling’s existing makeup instead of replacing unrelated cosmetic effects.

The Result

Painting follows the player’s finger more reliably, long strokes create less rendering work, and customization feels safer. The interaction preserves both unfinished decisions and previously applied cosmetics.

Preserving visual identity

The Problem

When hatchling artwork failed to resolve—or disappeared because mirroring was applied to the wrong visual layer—the same companion could look correct in its profile but unavailable or invisible elsewhere.

The Solution

I strengthened the shared hatchling artwork path and moved mirroring into a non-layout visual child. Makeup, colour effects, shiny effects, and other overlays now rely on the same resolved artwork before rendering.

The Result

The hatchling’s visual identity is more consistent across Nursery, Collection, customization, and gameplay contexts. This continuity supports recognition and attachment rather than making each screen feel disconnected.

11 — Visual & Interaction Decisions

Structuring the interface of the Enchanted Hatchling Journal around fantasy lore and tactical clarity. Here is how magical aesthetics meet premium layout engineering.

Hatchling-First Composition

The hatchling artwork remains the largest visual element because emotional attachment is the purpose of the profile. Identity appears before statistics, while element and mood icons provide quick recognition without requiring long explanations.

Identity Before Stats

Care values use labelled colour bars and icons, while battle information has its own compact panel. This separates nurturing decisions from combat progression. Primary actions use large icon-based targets suitable for portrait mobile play, and detailed or potentially destructive actions are visually separated from everyday care.

Glass-Like Panels

Glass-like panels, gold borders, soft glow, illustrated backgrounds, and gentle animation preserve the magical fantasy tone. These effects are used to establish hierarchy and feedback rather than simply adding decoration.

15 — Outcome

The iteration transformed the Hatchling Profile from a collection of competing features into a clearer companion-focused experience. Information is easier to scan, communication is less cluttered, the hatchling remains visually central, and customization interactions provide stronger feedback and protection against accidental loss. 

Automated project audits verify the current information paths, single Nursery speech area, artwork registration, makeup preservation, and brush-performance safeguards. No quantitative usability study has been completed, so the outcome is described through verified implementation improvements and documented interaction observations rather than unsupported performance or satisfaction claims. 

This work reinforced an important design lesson for me: polish is not only visual. A delightful interface also depends on predictable touch behaviour, clear information priorities, consistent character identity, and confidence that an action will produce the result the player expects.

16 — What I Learned

  • Designing isn't a straight line - UX doesn't end when the interface looks finished.
  • Every game state is part of the user experience. - I shaped and moved panels and cards a lot.
  • Navigation/scroll position had problems along with the Hitbox issues - A beautiful interface can still create friction if feedback isn't clear
  • Asset changes causing unexpected failures - I spent hours filtering through files to track down corrupted files.
