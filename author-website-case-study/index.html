Overview

An immersive author platform designed to connect storytelling, worldbuilding and e-commerce in one cohesive experience.

The challenge wasn't simply creating a beautiful fantasy website. It was organizing a large amount of interconnected content — books, characters, lore, artwork and purchasing paths — without overwhelming the reader.

THE CHALLENGE

The Dark Fantasy Paradox

The site needed to serve two very different types of visitors.New readers needed a clear path to discover a book, understand what it was about, read a sample and purchase it.

Existing readers wanted somewhere deeper to explore characters, lore, maps, artwork and the worlds behind the books.The UX challenge was finding a way to support both without turning the website into an overwhelming encyclopedia.

The Goals

Discover

Make books, samples and purchasing easy to find.

Explore

Give returning readers somewhere to dive deeper into the fictional worlds.

Trust

Present the author and her work professionally.

Immerse

Create a dark-fantasy atmosphere without sacrificing usability.

MY APPROACH

This was a content-driven project rather than a formal research study. I began by mapping the types of information visitors would need and separating essential actions from optional exploration.

II. Typographical Relics

A layered typography system. Rather than relying on a single decorative typeface, I created a four-level typography system where each font has a defined purpose.

Pirata One establishes the strongest visual identity and draws attention to primary content. Cinzel Decorative creates a secondary decorative layer for section introductions, while Allura adds softer, personal accents. Cormorant Garamond handles the majority of the reading experience, prioritising readability while maintaining the site's literary character.

This approach allowed the typography to feel distinctly dark-fantasy without sacrificing hierarchy, consistency, or usability.

Hero

Pirata one

Chosen for the hero and main H1 headings to immediately establish the website's dark-fantasy identity. Its dramatic gothic forms create a strong visual presence and evoke medieval storytelling, ancient manuscripts, and fantasy worlds.Using it only for major headings keeps its highly decorative character impactful without compromising readability throughout the rest of the site.

Accented & Decorative Text

Allura

Allura introduces a softer, more elegant contrast to the heavier gothic typography. Its flowing handwritten forms add a personal and romantic quality, reinforcing the author's identity and creating moments of visual warmth.It is used selectively for elements such as the author's name, buttons, and small decorative accents, allowing it to function as a visual detail rather than competing with the primary headings.

Eyebrow Headings

Cinzel Decorative

Selected for the small introductory headings placed above H1 and H2 sections. Its classical, ornamental letterforms complement Pirata One while providing a distinct layer within the typography hierarchy.This creates a subtle sense of structure and ceremony, helping introduce sections without relying on size alone to establish hierarchy.

Body text

Cormorant Garamond

Chosen for body copy because it combines the elegance of traditional serif typography with a refined, readable character. Its literary qualities support the author's storytelling identity while providing a clear contrast against the more decorative display fonts.
Using Cormorant Garamond for longer passages keeps the interface feeling cohesive with the fantasy aesthetic while prioritizing comfortable reading.

KEY DESIGN DECISIONS

The visual direction was only one part of the experience. These decisions shaped how readers discover characters, explore the world, and move from story discovery to purchasing.

01-CHARACTER EXPERIENCE

Reveal the story, not all at once.

The character archive was designed around progressive disclosure. Instead of presenting every detail immediately, each character begins with essential information before allowing readers to reveal more.

Basic information → Reveal more → Expanded character information

This approach keeps the archive approachable for readers who simply want to browse while giving more invested readers a deeper path into the characters.

It also creates a spoiler-safe experience: important lore and character details are progressively revealed rather than placing the full story context in front of the reader immediately.

The result is a character archive that supports different user needs — from casual discovery to deeper exploration.

  • Progressive Disclosure

02 - THE MAP & LORE

A world built to be explored.

The lore system was designed to make the fictional world feel like an experience rather than a collection of static pages.

The interaction follows a simple journey:
Lore Archive → Map → Marker → Lore Popup

Instead of separating worldbuilding from navigation, locations become entry points into the story. Readers can explore the world spatially, select a location, and discover the lore connected to it.

Worldbuilding wasn't treated as decoration. It became part of the information architecture.

This gives readers another way to navigate the story beyond the traditional page structure, while making the fictional world itself part of the interface.

  • Information Architecture

03 - E-COMMERCE EXPERIENCE

From discovery to checkout.

The website was designed to support book discovery and product selection without attempting to recreate the entire payment infrastructure.

The user journey is:
Book → Satchel → Payhip

Readers can discover a book within the branded website, add it to their Satchel, review their selections, and then continue to Payhip for secure checkout and delivery.

Rather than recreating payment processing, the website handles discovery and product selection while Payhip handles checkout and delivery.

This allowed the branded experience to continue through product discovery while keeping sensitive payment processing outside the custom site.

The result is a simpler architecture with a clearer responsibility for each part of the experience — while still giving the reader a cohesive journey from discovering a book to purchasing it.

  • Separation of Responsibilities

KEY DESIGN DECISIONS— PART II

04 - PRODUCT DECISION

THE DECISION TO REMOVE CHARACTER AI

Sometimes the best UX decision is removing a feature.

The original concept included generative AI characters that readers could interact with. While technically possible, the feature introduced significant complexity and ongoing cost without being essential to the core reader journey.

Rather than keeping the feature simply because it was innovative, I reassessed its actual value within the product.

Was Character AI helping readers discover and experience the books, or was it becoming a distraction from the experience itself?

The decision was to remove the generative AI concept and replace it with The Raven, a lightweight local-search experience that preserved the feeling of discovery without introducing the same technical complexity.

The decision wasn't about whether AI was technically possible. It was about whether the feature justified its complexity within the product.

This reduced unnecessary complexity while keeping the core experience focused on what mattered most: the books, characters, lore, and world.

05 - RESPONSIVE DESIGN

Designed to adapt, not simply shrink.

The responsive experience was designed around changing user needs rather than simply scaling the desktop layout down.

As the viewport becomes smaller, the hierarchy and interaction density change with it. Navigation collapses, content grids stack, CTA buttons expand, modal layouts adapt, and decorative effects are reduced where necessary.

The hero experience also adapts by replacing video with still imagery on smaller or constrained experiences, helping preserve the visual identity without making the interface unnecessarily heavy.

The mobile experience wasn't treated as a smaller desktop layout; the hierarchy and interaction density were deliberately adjusted for smaller screens.

The goal was to preserve the atmosphere of the original design while making the experience practical across different screen sizes and interaction contexts.

  • Navigation

    Collapses for smaller screens.

  • Content grids

    Transition from multi-column layout into stacked content

  • CTAs

    Expand to provide comfortable touch targets.

  • Hero media

    Video transitions to still imagery where appropriate.

  • Decorative effects

    Reduced to maintain performance and readability.

  • Modal layouts

    Adapt to smaller viewports.

  • Motion

    Reduced-motion preferences are supported.

06 - E-COMMERCE EXPERIENCE

Designing for confidence, not just aesthetics.

Accessibility and reader safety were treated as part of the experience rather than as legal or technical afterthoughts.

The interface includes considerations such as skip navigation, visible focus states, reduced-motion support, meaningful alt text, age-gate focus management, content warnings, and clearly accessible privacy and terms information.

These decisions are particularly important for a content-heavy author website because readers interact with more than visual storytelling. They need to understand where they are, what an interaction will do, and how the website handles their information.

The goal was not to remove the site's visual personality in the name of accessibility. Instead, accessibility was incorporated into the same visual system — maintaining the dark-fantasy atmosphere while making the experience clearer, more predictable, and more trustworthy.

Final Outcome

The final experience connects three different story worlds, a large character archive, lore, artwork and digital commerce within one reusable interface system.

Instead of forcing every visitor through the same journey, the experience lets readers choose their depth: discover a book, sample and purchase it, or step deeper into the worlds behind it.

  • 47 pages
  • 28 characters
  • 3 worlds

No conversion or engagement improvements are claimed because analytics and formal usability testing were not part of the project evidence.

WHAT I LEARNED

Immersion needs hierarchy.

Beautiful visuals are only useful when users can still understand where they are and what they can do.

Systems beat one-off pages.

As content grows, reusable components become essential to consistency and maintainability.

Product decisions matter.

Removing the character-AI concept taught me that good UX isn't about adding the most features — it's about protecting the experience that matters most.
